The person behind the threshold

Meet [Name]

For many years, [Name] worked as a vision therapist, helping patients strengthen and practice visual skills involved in reading, tracking, focusing, eye teaming, spatial awareness, and everyday function.

Her work now brings together professional experience, life in a hypermobile body, and a spiritual practice centered on autonomy, accessibility, and embodied self-trust.

Her years in vision therapy taught her that a person can have clear eyesight and still struggle with how comfortably or efficiently their visual system manages everyday tasks. They also showed her how often effort is mistaken for ease. Someone may complete a task while relying on intense concentration, physical tension, avoidance, or long recovery periods, even when that hidden cost is invisible to everyone around them.

Living with Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, hypermobility, and other chronic health conditions deepened that understanding. The body does not operate as a collection of unrelated parts. Pain can affect attention. Fatigue can reduce tolerance for visual demand. Joint instability can influence posture, coordination, and stamina. Sensory environments may become more difficult to navigate when several systems are already working overtime.

Between Sight & Shadow grew from a desire to make those overlapping experiences easier to understand. This website is not a replacement for optometry, physical therapy, occupational therapy, mental health care, or medical diagnosis. It is an educational space for noticing patterns, reducing unnecessary strain, preparing more useful questions, and exploring accommodations that respect fluctuating bodies.

What you will find here

This space offers accessible explanations of functional vision, practical approaches to visual and sensory strain, low-energy spiritual practices, printable tools, and reflections from someone who has experienced both helping others and learning to navigate a complicated body of her own.

The purpose is not to hand visitors one universal answer. It is to help people become more informed witnesses to their own experiences, recognize possible sources of strain, explore supportive accommodations, and understand when a symptom deserves evaluation from a qualified professional.
