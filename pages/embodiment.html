Complicated bodies deserve nuanced care

Embodiment without blame

Pacing, proprioception, sensory load, and supportive accommodations for hypermobile and chronically ill people.

Hypermobility is not simply flexibility

Joint hypermobility means a joint moves beyond the range expected for many people. It becomes clinically meaningful when it is associated with pain, instability, injury, fatigue, or disruption to daily life.

Ehlers-Danlos syndromes are a group of heritable connective-tissue disorders. Hypermobile EDS and hypermobility spectrum disorders can involve more than joints, and individual experiences vary widely. Diagnosis belongs with clinicians using current criteria and a full history, not a checklist on a website.

A body can look capable while using constant muscular effort to create stability. This is one reason “but you seem fine” can be so damaging. Visible range of motion does not reveal pain, dislocations, recovery time, dizziness, fatigue, or the cost of maintaining posture.

Proprioception and the map of the body

Proprioception is the nervous system’s sense of body position and movement. It helps you know where a limb is without looking directly at it. Joint laxity, pain, fatigue, and altered sensory feedback may complicate that internal map for some hypermobile people.

This can contribute to bumping into objects, using too much or too little force, feeling uncertain on stairs, bracing excessively, or relying heavily on vision for orientation. Because vision, balance, touch, and proprioception work together, strain in one area can change how demanding another task feels.

Important: Balance changes, falls, fainting, neurological symptoms, or rapidly changing coordination should be discussed with a qualified healthcare professional.

Pacing is not giving up

Pacing means matching activity to available capacity and planning recovery before the body reaches a crisis point. It may include breaking tasks into smaller parts, alternating positions, rotating between cognitive and physical work, scheduling rest, and using tools before pain becomes severe.

The goal is not perfect symptom control. It is reducing the boom-and-bust cycle in which a person does everything on a tolerable day and then loses several days to recovery.

A simple capacity check

  • What is my energy level before I begin?
  • Which part of this task creates the most strain?
  • Can I sit, support my arms, enlarge the text, reduce glare, or shorten the task?
  • What will tell me to pause before symptoms spike?
  • What recovery will I need afterward?

Sensory load is real work

Bright lights, visual clutter, layered sound, strong scents, motion, temperature changes, and social demands can accumulate. When pain, fatigue, migraine, dysautonomia, or visual strain are already present, a grocery store or waiting room can require significant neurological effort.

Accommodations might include tinted lenses prescribed for a specific need, hats or visors, quieter shopping hours, curbside pickup, ear protection, written instructions, seating, shorter appointments, reduced screen brightness, or permission to leave and return.

Not every tool suits every body. For example, overly dark lenses used indoors can be unhelpful for some visual or migraine concerns. Personalized advice is preferable when symptoms are persistent.

Movement should build support, not punishment

Some people with EDS or HSD benefit from individualized physical therapy that emphasizes stability, controlled strength, joint protection, balance, and proprioception. Stretching farther is not automatically helpful in a body that already has excessive range.

Yoga, tai chi, qigong, massage, mindfulness, and other complementary approaches may help some people manage aspects of pain or stress, but evidence varies by condition and practice. Adaptations, qualified instruction, and medical guidance matter, particularly when joints are unstable or other conditions are present.
