Finishing a task and completing it comfortably are not the same thing.

Success can hide compensation

In vision therapy, Kate repeatedly saw that a person could produce the expected answer while working far harder than anyone realized. Accuracy alone did not show how much tension, concentration, head movement, avoidance, or fatigue the task required.

Function includes sustainability

A useful question is not only whether a person can read, focus, track, or coordinate their eyes in one brief moment. It is whether they can do the task comfortably, consistently, at the required speed, and without an outsized recovery cost. This broader view applies far beyond vision.

Treatment claims need boundaries

Vision therapy is individualized clinical care for diagnosed visual conditions. It should not be marketed as a universal treatment for dyslexia, ADHD, or every reading problem. Research on convergence insufficiency, for example, supports improvement in specific clinical signs and symptoms while showing that gains should not be exaggerated into broad promises about reading comprehension.

Effort deserves documentation

People are often told they are functioning well because they can complete school, work, caregiving, or household tasks. Recording the time, symptoms, accommodations, and recovery involved reveals a more accurate picture. This is not pessimism. It is data about capacity.

Compassion changes the question

Instead of “Why can’t you just do it?” ask, “What is this task costing you, and what would make it more sustainable?” That question creates room for evaluation, accommodation, skill-building, and dignity.

When to seek care

This article provides general education, not diagnosis or treatment. New, severe, rapidly worsening, or sudden symptoms, especially sudden vision loss, neurological changes, fainting, severe pain, or symptoms that threaten safety, deserve prompt professional evaluation.
