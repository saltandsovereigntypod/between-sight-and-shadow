The Seeing Body

Vision is a whole-body task.

Plain-language education about visual skills, visual strain, and when to seek a comprehensive examination.

More than 20/20

Visual acuity describes how clearly a person sees detail at a particular distance. It does not fully describe how comfortably or efficiently the visual system performs during daily life.

Functional vision can include eye movement control, focusing, eye teaming, depth perception, visual attention, and the brain’s interpretation of visual information. These skills support tasks such as reading, copying, catching a ball, shifting between a screen and notes, moving through a crowded room, and maintaining orientation while the body is in motion.

A person can sometimes compensate for difficulty by slowing down, moving closer, closing one eye, using a finger to track text, avoiding near work, memorizing material, or pushing through discomfort. Compensation may hide the problem while increasing fatigue.

Visual skills in everyday life

Eye movements

Saccades are quick jumps of the eyes from one target to another, such as moving across words or scanning a shelf. Pursuits are smoother movements used to follow a moving target. Difficulty controlling these movements may show up as losing one’s place, skipping words, rereading, or feeling disoriented during motion.

Focusing

Accommodation helps the eyes adjust focus for different distances. Sustaining near focus, changing from near to far, or clearing blur quickly can become effortful for some people.

Eye teaming

Binocular vision is the coordinated use of both eyes. When the eyes do not aim together comfortably, symptoms may include eyestrain, headaches, intermittent blur or double vision, avoidance of reading, or reduced endurance. These symptoms have many possible causes and require professional evaluation.

Visual processing

Visual processing refers to how the brain organizes and interprets what the eyes deliver. It can involve spatial relationships, figure-ground discrimination, visual memory, form recognition, and integrating vision with movement. Educational descriptions of these skills should not be treated as a self-diagnosis.

Signs that deserve attention

  • Headaches or eye discomfort after near work
  • Blur that appears or worsens with sustained reading
  • Words that seem to move, swim, or overlap
  • Closing or covering one eye
  • Frequent loss of place while reading
  • Unusual fatigue after screens or paperwork
  • Motion sensitivity or discomfort in busy visual environments
  • Difficulty shifting focus between distances
  • New double vision, flashes, floaters, or vision loss
  • Avoidance that is mistaken for poor motivation
Urgent symptoms: Sudden vision loss, a curtain or shadow in the field of vision, new flashes with many floaters, severe eye pain, sudden double vision, or vision changes with neurological symptoms require prompt medical assessment.

What a comprehensive evaluation can clarify

A comprehensive eye and vision examination can evaluate eye health, refractive needs, visual acuity, eye alignment, focusing, and other aspects of visual function. Depending on the concern, a doctor of optometry may perform additional testing or refer to another professional.

Optometric vision therapy is an individualized sequence of neurosensory and neuromuscular activities prescribed and monitored by an optometrist after examination. It is not a generic collection of eye exercises, and exercises found online should not be used to treat unexplained symptoms without clinical guidance.

Questions you may bring to an appointment

  • Could my symptoms involve more than visual acuity?
  • Were eye teaming, focusing, and eye movements assessed?
  • Could my glasses prescription, dry eye, migraine, medication, or another condition contribute?
  • Would a developmental, behavioral, or neuro-optometric evaluation be appropriate?
  • What symptoms would require urgent follow-up?
  • What accommodations are safe while I wait for evaluation?

Gentle supports while seeking care

General comfort strategies may include taking regular visual breaks, enlarging text, adjusting screen distance, reducing glare, improving lighting, using a stable reading surface, changing posture, or alternating visual tasks with nonvisual tasks. Comfort strategies do not identify the cause of symptoms, but they may reduce avoidable demand.

The familiar “20-20-20” suggestion, briefly looking away from near work at intervals, may be comfortable for some people. It is not a treatment plan and should not be used to delay an examination when symptoms persist, worsen, or interfere with life.
